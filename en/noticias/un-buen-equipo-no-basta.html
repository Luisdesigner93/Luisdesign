A good tech team is not enough if no one sets the course

Bringing together talent and technical skills does not guarantee that a project will succeed.

Author
Luis Vicencio
Category
Digital product

Having good designers, developers and specialists is undoubtedly an advantage. But bringing together talent and technical skills does not guarantee that a project will succeed.

In many teams, the work moves forward: interfaces get designed, features get built, tools get implemented and tasks get done. Yet there is not always a shared question behind all that effort: what problem are we trying to solve?

When that answer is unclear, it is easy to mistake activity for progress. Solutions get built before the needs are understood, features get prioritized without grasping their impact, and processes get optimized that perhaps should never have existed.

The problem is not always a lack of organization. Sometimes, it is a lack of perspective.

Before executing, you have to understand.

Strategy is not simply about setting goals and assigning tasks. It means understanding the context, identifying opportunities, recognizing limitations and deciding where to focus the effort.

This requires connecting different perspectives: business, technology, design and people’s experience. Not to impose a direction from above, but to build a common understanding of what really matters.

Organizing is not the same as leading.

An organized backlog, an up-to-date roadmap or a well-defined workflow can help a team move forward. But none of these tools guarantees that we are moving in the right direction.

Product management, strategic design and technical coordination are valuable when they help us make better decisions, reduce uncertainty and learn along the way.

Otherwise, we risk turning organization into an end in itself.

Four phases, four questions.

The Harvard Business Review guide to project management organizes the work into four overlapping phases: planning, build-up, implementation and closeout. I read them as four questions a team should be able to answer:

  • Planning: what problem are we going to solve, and how will we know we solved it?
  • Build-up: do we have the people, the agreements and the plan to do it?
  • Implementation: is what we are learning changing our decisions?
  • Closeout: what are we delivering, who takes ownership and what did we learn?

The overlap is key: understanding the problem does not end when the build begins.

The real challenge is connecting capabilities.

A good team needs autonomy, knowledge and tools. But it also needs to understand how its work contributes to a shared purpose.

It is not about adding more meetings, processes or roles. It is about creating the conditions for decisions to make sense and for effort to turn into results.

Because developing faster does not always mean getting further. And having a technically excellent team is not worth much if we are solving the wrong problem.

The question is not only what we can build, but whether it is really worth building.

Reference: HBR Guide to Project Management. Harvard Business Review Press.
